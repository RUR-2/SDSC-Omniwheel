Abstract—This paper presents a novel framework for socially-
aware robot navigation in a pedestrian mixed environment. To
improve the acceptance of mobile robots in daily life, robots
should exhibit safe and socially acceptable motions to humans, as
integrating social norms has been shown to contribute to comfort
and socially-aware behavior in various studies. In this paper, we
propose a novel framework, Socially-aware Dynamical System
based Control (Social-DSC), which leverages the Dynamical
System based Control (DSC) for collision avoidance and Social
Force Model (SFM) for socially-aware robot navigation. While
safety is guaranteed by the DSC framework, the social norms
are considered in the modified SFM. In simulation studies,
we compared the proposed method with baseline methods,
demonstrating higher pedestrian path smoothness and reduced
effort in evading motion, thereby improving human comfort.

I. I NTRODUCTION
With the increasing demand for service and assistive
robots, it becomes essential for these robots to navigate in
a socially-acceptable manner. While safety is the primary
concern during robot navigation around humans, human
psychological aspects should be considered to enhance human
acceptance of mobile robots, necessitating the development of
Socially-aware Navigation (SAN). Recent survey works [1],
[2] indicate that integrating human-robot interaction (HRI) into
robot motion planning is crucial for SAN.
Existing approaches to SAN can be broadly categorized
into learning-based and rule-based methods. Learning-based
methods leverage machine learning-based techniques to model
HRI. For example, in deep learning based methods [3], [4],
reward functions that encode socially-aware terms are used to
train the robot control policy. The trained models can capture
complex and stochastic pedestrian behavior, but successful
training requires large and diverse datasets or simulators that
generate human trajectories using rule-based approaches [3],
and the lack of interpretability of the resulting model makes
it difficult to explicitly study the collision avoidance behavior.
Rule-based approaches model HRI through predefined rules
and integrate them into traditional navigation algorithms.
Proxemics theory [5] is one of the popular concepts of
social norm that studies human spatial behavior in social
contexts. In robotics, to respect social distance, the proxemic
zone is often modeled using potential functions, and an
optimization-based method is applied to find a suitable robot
trajectory, such as A* [6] or Timed Elastic Band [7]. Many
navigation frameworks based on proxemics focus on scenarios
where humans are stationary, and the computational speed
of optimization methods may be insufficient for dynamic
situations.
The Social Force Model (SFM) [8] is another widely studied
method that models interactions between pedestrians and their
environment as interaction forces. In [9], SFM has been
extended to incorporate interaction between human and robot,
and the force parameters are identified using experiment data.
[10] utilize the geometrical information of the environment to
calculate a preferred gap for robot navigation and incorporate
it into the SFM. [11] extended SFM with the robot’s physical
touch force to interact with the humans to solve the freezing
robot problem. The challenge of deploying SFM to robots
lies in the necessity of carefully calibrating hand-designed
parameters to accommodate different scenarios [9]. Moreover,
while SFM implicitly reflects safety distance through repulsive
force, it does not inherently guarantee collision avoidance [12].
Recently, SFM-based HRI models have been embedded into
various frameworks for socially-aware navigation, such as A*
[12], RRT [13] and dynamic window approach [11].
We propose the Social-DSC, a novel framework combining
the principles of SFM with Dynamical Systems based Control
(DSC). DSC is a closed-form approach for robot collision
avoidance, which ensures safety and convergence to goal
positions [14], [15]. The capability of DSC for collision
avoidance has been qualitative evaluated in pedestrian-rich
environments [14], [16]. Notably, our work extends DSC by
incorporating SFM to influence the robot motion, and we
evaluate the proposed framework by comparing it with the
baseline methods in SAN. To our knowledge, the application
of DSC in the SAN context has not been previously studied.
This paper is organized as follows. Section III covers the
problem setup and the preliminaries of the DSC collision
avoidance. Section IV introduces the proposed Social-DSC
method. Section V presents our simulation results and Section
VI concludes with remarks.

A. Problem Statement
Consider a mobile robot r, which follows its nominal
dynamics f (ξr) : R2 7→ R2 to accomplish a navigation task:
˙ξr = f (ξr) (1)
where ξr(t) ∈ R2 denotes the robot position at time t.
We assume that there is a high-level trajectory planner that
calculates the nominal dynamics f (ξr) for the robot to guide it
towards the navigation goal point in the absence of pedestrians.
When encountering unexpected obstacles and pedestrians,
the robot should replan its motion while keeping the motion
as close as possible to the nominal dynamics. By means of
the DSC based collision avoidance algorithm [14], the robot
control input can be computed as
˙ξr = M (ξr)f (ξr) (2)
where M (ξr) ∈ R2×2 denotes the modulation matrix,
detailed in Section III-B. However, DSC depends solely on
the geometric relationships between nominal dynamics and
the obstacle shapes. In addition, social awareness needs to
be considered to avoid human discomfort. This work aims to
develop an algorithm that adapts the nominal dynamics f (ξr)
in Eq. 1, ensuring both safety and human comfort.

λv ≈ 0, allowing modulation to take effect. When robot moves
away from the obstacle, λv ≈ 1, causing the modulation
matrix to become an identity matrix. This ensures that the
robot does not conform to the shape of the obstacle when
moving away, thereby maintaining a natural and efficient
motion.

Figure 2 shows the architecture of the proposed Social-DSC
method. The input to the Social-DSC is the nominal dynamics
f (ξr), determined by the Global Planner block. Inspired by
the formulation in [15], the Social-DSC is given as:
˙ξr = Msaf ety(ξr) (f (ξr) + fSIM (ξr)) (10)
The concept of Social-DSC can be explained as the following
steps. First, the modulation matrice Msaf ety(ξr) is computed
for individual obstacles and the proxemic zones of pedestrians
(Section IV-B). Then, an additional dynamics fSIM (ξr)
is generated by the Social Inference Model block which
generates a velocity vector that guides the robot’s movement
in a manner that aligns with social behaviors (Section IV-C).
Finally, the Eq. 10 is applied to ensure that the resulting motion
avoids collisions with obstacles and maintains a socially
acceptable distance from pedestrians.
A. Modeling of Human Proxemic Zone
To integrate human spatial behavior into DSC framework,
we utilize the proxemics theory, which formalizes the space
around a pedestrian into four zones: intimate zone, personal
zone, social zone and public zone [5]. The social zone refers
to the distance that people maintain during social interactions
to respect for personal space. In [17], this zone is defined to
range from 1.22m to 3.66m for human-robot interaction.
We model the proxemic zone of pedestrian i as an equi-
potential contour of the asymmetric Gaussian potential field
[18] fag,i(ξr; ξp,i, θp,i, σi,h, σi,l, σi,r) : R2 7→ R>0 (Fig. 1a).
The parameters ξp,i, θp,i denote the position and orientation of
the pedestrian, and σi,h, σi,l, σi,r are the variances along the
heading, lateral and rear directions, respectively. The proxemic
zone is defined as the region bounded by the level line
fag,i(ξp,i + σi,hu(θp,i)). To balance between robot motion
efficiency and human comfort, we empirically choose the
values of the variances so that the contour shape represents
the personal zone defined in [17], ensuring that the minimum
distance between the robot and pedestrians ranges between
1.22m and 3.66m (see Section V). The contour shape is
intuitively convex. 2

V. S IMULATION AND EVALUATION
We conducted simulations in ROS using pedsim ros
package [19], where the simulated pedestrians are driven by
SFM to interact with obstacles, other pedestrians and the robot.
We compare Social-DSC with DSC and baseline rule-based
methods in SAN: SFM [9], ORCA [20]. An omni-directional
mobile robot simulator is used for the evaluation. The
simulations are conducted on a PC with an Intel i7-6820HQ
CPU and 16GB RAM.

A. Simulation Setup
The robot reference velocities are set to vr,ref = 1.2m/s,
the controllers’ sampling time is 0.1s. The SFM controller
adopts the identified parameters in [9] for human-robot
interaction, and we empirically set Ao,i = 1 to reduce obstacle
influences, while keeping other parameters for obstacle the
same as human model. For the ORCA controller, the prediction
time horizon is 2s. The radii of robot and pedestrian are set
to 0.4m and 1m, respectively, and the robot takes half of
the responsibility for collision avoidance. For the DSC, the
nominal dynamics is f (ξr) = (ξ∗ − ξr), where ξ∗ is a static
target position. Based on [18], the parameters are adjusted to
σh = ∆T ∥vi∥ , σr = 12 σh, where ∥vp∥ = 1.2m/s, ∆T = 2s
are the estimated average pedestrian speed and prediction time,
respectively. σl is set to 1.2m due to the narrow corridor
environment. The Social-DSC uses the same DSC and SFM
parameters. kSIM is set to 0.3, ksr,i = 1 and δi = 0.2. The
SIM forces are equally weighted.
The controllers are evaluated in two scenarios: a narrow
corridor collision avoidance and a four-agent position-
swapping task. In each scenario, agents travel from its start
to goal position, with 100 simulation iterations conducted.
Pedestrians are driven by default SFM parameters in
pedsim ros, and are randomly spawned within a 1m2 area
around their initial positions at each iteration. Their velocities
are also randomly assigned between 0.26m/s and 1.34m/s
by default in pedsim ros.

VI. C ONCLUSION AND OUTLOOKS
In this paper, we present the Soical-DSC, a novel approach
that combines the DSC with the SFM to generate safe and
socially-aware robot trajectories. Compared to the baseline
methods, our approach generates robot motions that explicitly
consider the social distance. The proposed method enhances
human comfort, as evidenced by the higher smoothness of
human paths and a lower path ratio. Future work involve
conducting real-world experiments and incorporating adaptive
behaviors to enhance the interaction model.
